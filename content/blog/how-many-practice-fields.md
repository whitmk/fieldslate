# How Many Practice Fields Does Your League Need? A Guide for Volunteer Boards

*Last updated: October 2026. By Whit Mellon-King, founder of FieldSlate and a Little League board member.*

Every winter, somebody on a league board gets the email from the city: *field requests are due.* And every winter, that person opens a spreadsheet, stares at last year's numbers, and guesses.

Guessing low means teams practicing in the outfield of a game in progress, or not at all. Guessing high means paying for fields that sit empty, and a city parks department that remembers. This guide walks through how to get the number right, and there's a [free practice field calculator](https://www.thefieldslate.com/tools/practice-field-calculator) that does the math for you.

## The mistake most leagues make: counting hours

The natural instinct is to add up practice time. Fifty teams, two practices a week, ninety minutes each: 150 field hours a week. Divide by the hours a field is available, and there's your answer.

The problem is that cities don't grant field *hours*. They grant fields on specific nights during specific windows. And your teams don't spread themselves evenly across the week. If most of your divisions can only practice between 5 and 8pm on weeknights, then what matters is **how many teams need a field at the same time on your busiest night.**

That's the number to request. Everything else is detail.

## The five numbers you need for each division

**1. How many teams.** The multiplier everything hangs on. Use your best estimate from registration; you can always adjust.

**2. Practices per team per week, and how long.** One 60-minute practice for T-ball looks very different from two 2-hour practices for Juniors.

**3. Which days and what hours.** Be honest about the window. If coaches can't get there before 5:30 because of work, the window starts at 5:30, not whatever the city allows. And leave game days out: a field hosting a Saturday doubleheader isn't free for practice.

**4. What size field.** This is the one people forget. A Majors field can't be split for Juniors, and a Juniors team can't practice on a T-ball diamond. In Little League terms, you're really counting three separate pools: 60-foot diamonds, 50/70 diamonds, and 90-foot diamonds. Count each one separately.

**5. How many teams can share a field.** Younger divisions often split a diamond, one team on each half. That alone can cut T-ball and coach-pitch field needs in half.

## Spread evenly versus everyone on Tuesday

Here's the part that surprises boards. Take an example league with seven divisions, mostly practicing weeknights between 5 and 8pm. If the league assigns practice nights so every night carries the same load, it needs **6** sixty-foot diamonds.

If coaches pick their own nights, and they all pick Tuesday and Thursday the way coaches do, the same league needs **14**.

Same teams, same practices. More than double the fields. The gap between those two numbers is a decision for your board: either assign practice nights, or request more fields. The calculator shows both so you can make that call with real numbers instead of hope.

## The daylight problem

Spring seasons start in the dark. Daylight saving time doesn't begin until the second Sunday in March, and in late February sunset can come before 6pm across much of the country.

So a practice window that runs until 8pm isn't really an 8pm window on February 22nd. In Sacramento, for example, sunset that day is about 5:52pm. A division whose practices end at 6pm can go without lights from around March 3rd. A division finishing at 8pm can't practice on an unlit field until early May.

Check this before you submit. If your early-season practices depend on lit fields, say so in the request, or plan shorter early-season windows. Fall seasons have the same problem in reverse: the windows that work in August stop working in October.

## Turning the number into a city request

A good request tells the parks department exactly what you need, so they don't have to guess either:

- **Field size and count**, separately for each size.
- **The nights you need them**, and how many on each night.
- **The time window** on each night.
- **The season dates.**

Something like: *"60-foot diamonds: 6 fields Monday through Friday, 5pm to 8pm, February 22 to May 29."* If the honest answer is different from last year, a one-line explanation helps ("two new Minors teams this spring").

## FAQ

**How many practice fields does a youth baseball league need?**
It depends on how many teams practice at the same time on your busiest night, not on your total practice hours. Count teams per division, practices per team per week, practice length, the days and hours teams can practice, and whether younger divisions can share a field. Then find the night where the most fields are in use at once. That number, by field size, is what to request.

**Should we request fields based on total practice hours?**
No. Total hours tell you how much field time you use across the week, but cities grant fields by night and time window. A league can have plenty of total hours and still run out of fields on Tuesday at 5:30. Request based on your busiest night.

**Can two teams share one practice field?**
Often, for younger divisions. T-ball and coach-pitch teams commonly split a field, one team on each half. That can cut those divisions' field needs in half. Older divisions usually need the whole field.

**Why does daylight matter for spring practice fields?**
Most spring seasons start before daylight saving time, when sunset can be before 6pm. A practice window that runs until 8pm may only be usable until about 6pm in late February unless the field has lights. Check when each division's practices actually end against sunset on your first day of practice.
