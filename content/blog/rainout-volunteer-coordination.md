# One Rainout, Fourteen Messages: Why Volunteer Coordination Breaks When the Schedule Changes

It's 6:40 on a Saturday morning, it rained all night, and the field at the 9:00 Majors game is a lake. Calling the game takes ten seconds. Telling everyone takes the rest of the morning.

If you've been the person holding the phone, you know the feeling: the game is canceled, but the work has just started. Every person tied to that one game needs to hear about it, and if one of them doesn't, the cost lands on a parent, a kid or a volunteer who showed up for nothing.

This post is about that cascade: who actually has to be told when one game changes, what happens when one message gets missed, and why the usual tools make it worse.

## Who has to hear about one game

Take that one rained-out Majors game, home team against visitors. Here's who a typical volunteer-run league has to reach, and it adds up to fourteen messages:

- **Head coaches (2):** They tell their players and parents.
- **Team parents (2):** They run the team group chats and the snack lists.
- **Umpires (2):** Plate and base, often teenagers whose parents drive them.
- **GameChanger scorekeepers (2):** One per team, each a parent volunteer.
- **Field prep volunteer (1):** Lines the field an hour before the game.
- **Announcer (1):** Runs the PA and walk-up music.
- **Snack shack volunteers (2):** The shift was built around this game.
- **Field or city contact (1):** The permit, the lights, the gate code.

That's just the cancellation. When the makeup date is set, every one of those fourteen people needs a second message with the new time, and each one has to check whether they can still make it.

## What one missed message costs

Fourteen messages, sent by a tired volunteer on a Saturday morning, is fourteen chances for one to slip. And each slip looks small until you see where it lands:

- **The scorekeeper who didn't hear about the makeup** isn't there, so nobody tracks pitch counts, and a coach has to guess whether a pitcher is eligible next game.
- **The field-prep volunteer who did hear about the cancellation, but not the move** shows up at 8:00 the next Saturday to an empty field, and probably doesn't volunteer again.
- **The umpire who wasn't told** means a coach calls balls and strikes, and both dugouts spend the game arguing about it.
- **The snack shack family who missed it** opens the shack for a game that isn't happening, or worse, doesn't open it for the one that is.

None of these is a disaster on its own. But volunteer-run leagues run on goodwill, and goodwill wears thin fast. The parent who stood at an empty field is the parent who says no next season.

## Why sign-up sheets can't keep up

Most leagues handle volunteers with a general-purpose sign-up tool like SignUpGenius. It's a good tool, and it's free, which is why so many boards use it. But it has one blind spot that matters every time it rains: **the sign-up sheet doesn't know your schedule.**

When a parent signs up for "Saturday, 9:00, GameChanger," that's a line of text on a sheet. When the game moves to Tuesday at 6:00, the sheet still says Saturday at 9:00. Someone has to remember the sheet exists, edit the slot, and email the parent. Multiply that by every role on every moved game, and the sheet drifts away from the real schedule within a few weeks of the season.

The same problem repeats for umpires (a group text), the snack shack (a paper calendar) and the schedule itself (a spreadsheet). Four tools, four copies of the truth, and one volunteer coordinator trying to keep them in sync by hand.

## The fix: tie every spot to the game

The answer isn't a better reminder system. It's making the volunteer spot part of the game, so there's only one copy of the truth to change.

That's how we built volunteer sign-ups into [FieldSlate](https://www.thefieldslate.com). A GameChanger or field-prep spot isn't a line on a separate sheet; it belongs to a specific game on the schedule. So when that game changes:

1. **It moves:** the spot moves with it, and the volunteer gets an email with the new time and a link to keep the spot or cancel.
2. **It's rained out:** the volunteer keeps the spot and gets an email saying so. When the makeup date is set, they get another with the new time.
3. **The rainout is called off:** they get a "back on" email.

The same applies to snack shack shifts, which are built from the game times, and to umpires, who are assigned to the game itself. The coordinator makes one change, and the volunteers signed up for that game hear about it on their own.

Families sign up from one link. Everyone else sees a filled spot as "Taken"; only the league sees names. And because every spot is tied to a real game, the league can see at a glance which families have worked their shifts this season.

## A rainout checklist for any board

Whatever tools your league uses, these five habits cut the misses:

1. **Write down everyone tied to a game,** once, before the season. Use the table above as a starting point; your league may have more roles.
2. **Keep one source of truth for the schedule.** If volunteers sign up somewhere else, someone owns keeping that sheet in sync, by name.
3. **Send the makeup date the same way you sent the cancellation.** The second message is the one that gets missed.
4. **Ask volunteers to confirm the new time,** not just acknowledge the cancellation. A spot nobody confirmed is an open spot.
5. **Track who worked, not who signed up.** No-shows tell you which roles need a backup next season.
